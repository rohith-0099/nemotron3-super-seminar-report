\chapter{Literature Survey}
\label{ch:survey}

\NS\ does not introduce a single new idea so much as bring several existing
ones together at a larger scale than before. This chapter traces those ideas
through the literature in roughly chronological order and identifies the gap
that each work left open.

\section{Transformers and Their Cost}

Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} proposed the Transformer, a network
built solely on attention, without recurrence or convolution. Because attention
relates all pairs of positions in one step, the model is highly parallel during
training and captures long-range dependencies well. The same property makes its
cost quadratic in the sequence length. Later engineering reduced the constant
factors without removing the scaling: Grouped-Query Attention
(GQA)~\cite{ainslie2023gqa}, for instance, lets several query heads share one
key--value head, which shrinks the KV cache considerably while keeping quality
close to that of full multi-head attention. \NS\ still uses GQA in the few
attention layers it retains.

\section{State-Space Models: S4, Mamba and Mamba-2}

Gu et al.~\cite{gu2021s4} showed with the Structured State Space sequence
model (S4) that a linear state-space system, discretised and suitably
parameterised, can model sequences tens of thousands of steps long. S4's
dynamics, however, were the same for every input, which limited it on
information-dense data such as text.

Mamba~\cite{gu2023mamba} removed this limitation by making the state-space
parameters \emph{functions of the input}. This ``selection mechanism'' lets the
model decide, token by token, what to write into its state and what to ignore
(Figure~\ref{fig:mamba-selective}). Its authors report Mamba as the first linear-time model to
match Transformers of similar size on language modelling.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{mamba-fig01-selective-ssm.pdf}
  \caption[Selective state-space model with input-dependent parameters]{Selective
  state-space model: each channel of the input $x$ is mapped to the output $y$
  through a latent state $h$, and a selection mechanism makes the SSM
  parameters depend on the input. \source{Gu and Dao (2023)~\cite{gu2023mamba}, Fig.~1}}
  \label{fig:mamba-selective}
\end{figure}

Dao and Gu~\cite{dao2024mamba2} then developed the \emph{structured state space
duality} (SSD) framework, showing that SSMs and certain forms of attention are
two views of the same class of structured matrices. The resulting Mamba-2 layer
is a refinement of Mamba's selective SSM whose core computation is 2--8$\times$
faster than Mamba's, while remaining competitive with Transformers on language
modelling. Its remaining weakness is inherent to the approach: the state is a
compressed summary, so exact details from far back in the sequence can fade.

\section{Hybrid Mamba--Transformer Models}

If Mamba is efficient but forgetful and attention is precise but expensive, the
natural next step is to mix them. Jamba~\cite{lieber2024jamba} from AI21 Labs
was the first large model to do so, interleaving blocks of Transformer and
Mamba layers and adding MoE to some of them (Figure~\ref{fig:jamba}). It showed
strong results for contexts of up to 256K tokens while fitting on a single
80\,GB GPU, and is the closest outside comparison to the Nemotron design.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{jamba-fig01-block.pdf}
  \caption[A Jamba block mixing attention, Mamba and MoE layers]{(a) A single
  Jamba block and (b) the layer types inside it: attention or Mamba followed by
  an MLP or MoE module. \source{Lieber et al.\ (2024)~\cite{lieber2024jamba}, Fig.~1}}
  \label{fig:jamba}
\end{figure}

Waleffe et al.~\cite{waleffe2024empirical} at NVIDIA carried out the controlled
comparison that the field had been missing. They trained 8-billion-parameter
Mamba, Mamba-2 and Transformer models on the same data (up to 3.5 trillion
tokens) and found that pure SSMs match or exceed Transformers on many tasks but
lag on tasks that need strong copying or in-context learning, such as five-shot
MMLU and a ``Phonebook'' lookup task. A hybrid made of 43\% Mamba-2, 7\%
attention and 50\% MLP layers closed that gap: it exceeded the Transformer on
all twelve standard tasks evaluated (by 2.65 points on average) and was
predicted to be up to 8$\times$ faster when generating tokens. Their ablation
on the proportion of attention layers (Figure~\ref{fig:attn-ratio}) found that
validation loss is lowest when only about 8\% of the layers are attention
layers. This is the core idea behind this seminar: \emph{a few attention layers
are enough}.

\section{The Nemotron Lineage}

NVIDIA turned this finding into a product line.
\emph{Nemotron-H}~\cite{nvidia2025nemotronh} was a family of 8B and 56B/47B
hybrid models in which roughly 8\% of the layers are self-attention layers,
evenly dispersed, and the rest alternate between Mamba-2 and feed-forward
layers (Figure~\ref{fig:nemotronh}). These models matched or exceeded
similarly sized Transformers such as Qwen-2.5 and Llama-3.1 while being up to
3$\times$ faster at inference. The paper also introduced an FP8 training
recipe, a first step towards the low-precision training used later.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{nemotronh-fig02-layer-pattern.pdf}\\[6pt]
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{nemotronh-fig01-throughput.pdf}
  \caption[Nemotron-H layer pattern and accuracy versus throughput]{Top:
  Nemotron-H-8B/56B layer patterns, with attention layers evenly dispersed
  among alternating Mamba-2 and FFN layers. Bottom: MMLU-Pro accuracy against
  per-GPU inference throughput for the larger models. \source{NVIDIA
  (2025)~\cite{nvidia2025nemotronh}, Figs.~2 and~1}}
  \label{fig:nemotronh}
\end{figure}

\emph{Nemotron Nano~2}~\cite{nvidia2025nano2} applied the Nemotron-H
architecture to a reasoning model. A 12B base model was pretrained on 20
trillion tokens with the FP8 recipe and then compressed to 9B parameters; it
reached up to 6$\times$ higher throughput than Qwen3-8B in reasoning settings
with long generated ``thinking'' traces, exactly where constant-memory
generation pays off.

The \emph{Nemotron~3} white paper~\cite{nvidia2025nemotron3} then announced a
family of three models---Nano, Super and Ultra---that add Mixture-of-Experts to
the hybrid backbone and support contexts of up to one million tokens.
\emph{\NN}~\cite{nvidia2025nano3}, the first to be released, has 31.6B
parameters of which 3.2B are active; its MoE layers activate 6 of 128 experts.
It achieved up to 3.3$\times$ higher throughput than GPT-OSS-20B and
Qwen3-30B-A3B while being more accurate on popular benchmarks
(Figure~\ref{fig:nano3}). Nano, however, used a standard MoE and was trained in
ordinary BF16 precision.

\section{Supporting Techniques}

Three further works supply the remaining ingredients of \NS.

\textbf{Mixture-of-Experts.} Shazeer et al.~\cite{shazeer2017moe} introduced
the sparsely-gated MoE layer, and GShard~\cite{lepikhin2020gshard} scaled it to
very large Transformers with automatic sharding and a load-balancing loss.
DeepSeek-V3~\cite{deepseek2024v3} popularised fine-grained experts with shared
experts and auxiliary-loss-free load balancing in an open model.
LatentMoE~\cite{elango2026latentmoe} revisits the design from a
hardware--software co-design perspective and moves the experts into a
lower-dimensional latent space; it is the MoE variant adopted in \NS.

\textbf{Multi-token prediction.} Gloeckle et al.~\cite{gloeckle2024mtp}
showed that training a model to predict several future tokens at once, using
extra output heads on a shared trunk, improves sample efficiency and enables
faster inference (Figure~\ref{fig:mtp-meta}). Speculative
decoding~\cite{leviathan2022speculative,chen2023speculative} is the inference
technique that turns such cheap extra predictions into real speed-ups without
changing the output distribution.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{mtp-fig01-overview.png}
  \caption[Multi-token prediction with several output heads on a shared trunk]{Multi-token
  prediction: several output heads on top of a shared trunk predict the next
  $n$ tokens. \source{Gloeckle et al.\ (2024)~\cite{gloeckle2024mtp}, Fig.~1}}
  \label{fig:mtp-meta}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Low-precision training.} NVIDIA's NVFP4
paper~\cite{nvidia2025nvfp4} demonstrated stable pretraining of a 12B model in
a 4-bit floating-point format over 10 trillion tokens, with a loss that tracks
an FP8 baseline closely. \NS\ is the first Nemotron~3 model to use this recipe
for its full pretraining run.
